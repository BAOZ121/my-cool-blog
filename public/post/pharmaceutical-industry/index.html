The Pharmaceutical Industry: History, Structure, and Challenges

A deep dive into the 150-year evolution of the pharmaceutical industry, core market structures, and future challenges.

2026-08-21 11 minutes read

Part 1: Story

In the early winter of 1960, a 46-year-old female reviewer named Frances Kelsey joined the U.S. FDA (Food and Drug Administration).

On her desk lay a seemingly ordinary new drug application—thalidomide. This drug was wildly popular in Europe, hailed as a “miracle drug” for pregnant women relieving nausea. The developing company and its executives were pressuring her to approve it before Christmas.

But Kelsey withstood all political and commercial pressure because she noticed that the report lacked a small section on neurotoxicological data regarding the drug’s ability to cross the placenta. She refused to sign it.

Just one year later, tragedy struck globally: tens of thousands of babies in Europe and Japan were born with phocomelia, short limbs resembling those of a seal. The United States, however, miraculously escaped this fate thanks to Kelsey’s insistence.

This tragedy directly led to the 1962 Kovwa-Harris Amendment, which stipulated that any new drug must prove it is “not only safe but also effective” before being marketed.

This single decree completely altered the global pharmaceutical industry’s business landscape. It effectively ended the era of chaotic, unregulated growth, forcibly increasing the costs and time of research and development—new drug development transformed from “a few years, a few million dollars” into a “10 years, a billion dollars” gamble, turning the pharmaceutical industry into a “high-barrier gold mine” monopolized by a very few giants. Behind this lies a vast industry: the pharmaceutical industry itself.

Part 2: Industry History

The modern pharmaceutical industry has a history of less than 150 years, but its development has been extremely complex. Its evolution can be summarized as follows: the research paradigm shifted from “random trial and error” to “precise programming”; regulatory thresholds changed from “unregulated growth” to “extremely high compliance”; and the business ecosystem evolved from “chemical conglomerates” to “large pharmaceutical companies + biotechnology synergy.” Here, my research shows that most pharmaceutical manufacturing occurred in the United States and Europe.

1. Late 19th Century - 1930s: From Dye Workshops to Chemical Synthesis (Disorderly Emergence)

Plant Extraction and Accidental Discovery.

In 1899, the German dye giant Bayer launched Aspirin.

Modern pharmaceuticals were born in the chemical and textile industries. Scientists, while developing synthetic dyes, discovered that certain coal tar derivatives and azo dyes had bactericidal or analgesic effects. At that time, German dye giants such as IG Farben naturally entered the pharmaceutical industry. At this time, the industry had almost no concept of “clinical trials,” and drug sales were similar to folk remedies.

2. 1940s - 1960s: Mass Production of Antibiotics and the Iron Curtain of Regulation (Foundational Period)

Bacterial Culture and Large-Scale Random Screening.

During World War II, Pfizer industrialized the mass production of penicillin using deep fermentation technology; in 1962, the U.S. passed the Cofvale-Harris Amendment (due to the thalidomide teratogenicity incident involving thalidomide).

Large-scale antibiotic production laid the foundation for modern pharmaceutical companies’ fermentation and large-scale purification capabilities. More importantly, the thalidomide tragedy forcibly established a legal iron curtain requiring drugs to demonstrate efficacy and safety before being marketed. This directly raised the industry’s R&D funding threshold, weeding out small chemical plants lacking capital and forming the initial prototype of an oligopoly.

3. 1970s - 1990s: The Era of Molecular Biology and Blockbuster Drugs (The Golden Age of Profits)

Target-Based Drug Discovery.

The lipid-lowering drug Lipitor and the antidepressant Prozac were launched.

With a better understanding of cell receptors and enzymes, R&D no longer relied on blind trial and error, but rather on “designing small chemical molecules targeting lesion receptors.” Targeting the vast population suffering from chronic diseases such as hypertension and hyperlipidemia, pharmaceutical companies have developed a “blockbuster” model with annual sales exceeding $10 billion. Extremely high profit margins (>80%) have attracted massive global capital investment, with Big Pharma companies like Pfizer, Merck, and Bristol-Myers Squibb establishing their global commercial dominance.

4. 2000s - 2010s: The Biomolecular Revolution and Restructuring of Specialization

Recombinant DNA technology and monoclonal antibodies (mAbs).

The blockbuster drug “Humira” was launched; the “Eroom’s Law” (anti-Moore’s Law) emerged (i.e., doubling R&D investment resulted in a decrease in new drug output).

Easy-to-drug chemical small molecule targets were almost exhausted. The rise of biotech companies, represented by Genentech, ushered in the era of biopharmaceuticals. Large pharmaceutical companies (Big Pharma) have undergone a fundamental transformation in their business models due to bloated organizations and low innovation efficiency—shifting from full-process in-house R&D to “acquisition/license-in.” This has established a modern dual-helix division of labor: “Biotech companies handle forward-looking research and risk-taking trial and error, while Big Pharma companies handle late-stage clinical trials and global sales.”

5. 2020s to Present: Multimodal, Precision Medicine, and Computational Drug Development

Programmable drugs (mRNA, ADC, CGT) and AI-driven computing (AIDD).

mRNA vaccines were rapidly developed and launched during the COVID-19 crisis; AI structural biology tools such as AlphaFold have achieved widespread penetration; ADCs (antibody-drug conjugates) have become the mainstay of precision oncology.

Drug discovery is shifting from traditional “wet experimental trial and error” to “dry and wet combined digital programming.” Treatment of complex diseases such as cancer is increasingly focusing on microscopic gene mutations (precision medicine), and the traditional broad-based “blockbuster” model is being supplemented by high-priced, small-group precision drugs targeting specific gene targets.

Part 3: Industry Structure

The pharmaceutical industry is a complex and unique industry characterized by high technological barriers, high compliance thresholds, long cycles, high profit margins, and high risks. The entire industry chain can be clearly divided into three core segments: upstream (R&D support), midstream (pharmaceutical companies and product portfolios), and downstream (distribution, channels, and payers).

I. Upstream: R&D and Production Support (“Water Sellers” and Infrastructure)

The upstream provides pharmaceutical companies with comprehensive services from target discovery and experimental consumables to contract manufacturing. It is a relatively risk-dispersed and cash-flow-stable “water-selling” segment. It is divided into four complex parts:

  1. Scientific Reagents and High-End Instruments

    • Core Functions and Roles: Providing gene sequencers, high-resolution mass spectrometers, culture media, biochips, and laboratory consumables; it is the “arsenal” of pharmaceutical R&D.
    • Representative companies/institutions: Thermo Fisher Scientific, Danaher, Merck KGaA, Illumina

  2. Active Pharmaceutical Ingredients (APIs) & Intermediates

    • Core Functions and Roles: Providing active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) and key chemical intermediates. Divided into bulk APIs (such as vitamins and antibiotics) and specialty/high-difficulty APIs.
    • Representative companies/institutions: Zhejiang Medicine, Huahai Pharmaceutical, Lonza, Teva (API division)

  3. CXO (Contract Research and Development Organization)

    • Core Functions and Roles: CRO (Contract Research Organization) assists in preclinical and clinical trials; CDMO/CMO (Contract Development and Manufacturing Organization) assists in process development and commercial mass production.
    • Representative companies/institutions: WuXi AppTec, Pharmaron, Tigermed, Lonza, Catalent

  4. AI Computing Power and Cutting-Edge Technology Platform

    • Core Functions and Roles: Provides gene editing tools, protein structure prediction, and AI drug molecular screening platform (AIDD).
    • Representative companies/institutions: Schrödinger, Recursion Pharma, Insilico Medicine

II. Midstream: Pharmaceutical Companies and Product Matrix (Core Value Creators and Risk Bearers)

The midstream is the main body of the pharmaceutical industry, bearing the most significant financial risks in drug development and being the primary beneficiaries of patent monopoly premiums. It can be broken down into four camps based on business models:

  1. Big Pharma (Multinational Traditional Giants)

    • Business Model: In-house research + external mergers and acquisitions (M&A) / licensing introduction.
    • Core Barriers: Extensive global clinical trial compliance capabilities, a global commercial sales network, and abundant cash flow.
    • Representative companies: Pfizer, Eli Lilly, Novartis, Merck (MSD), AstraZeneca.

  2. Biotech (Innovative Biotechnology Companies)

    • Business Model: Focuses on specific cutting-edge targets or new technology platforms (such as ADC, mRNA, CAR-T). They typically lack a mature sales force, and after reaching Phase II clinical trials, they often choose to sell their rights to a Big Pharma or be directly acquired.
    • Core Barriers: The R&D efficiency of top scientists and patent protection.
    • Representative companies: BioNTech, Moderna, BeiGene, Sarepta.

  3. Generic Pharma (Generic Drug Companies)

    • Business Model: Involves rapidly following up on original drug patents after they expire (Paragraph IV challenge), achieving large-scale substitution at extremely low cost.
    • Core Barriers: Extremely high production cost control, ability to tackle complex formulations, and rapid supply chain response capabilities.
    • Representative companies: Teva, Sandoz, Viatris, Sun Pharma.

  4. Specialty Pharma

    • Business Model: Avoid the fierce competition of Big Pharma in areas like oncology and cardiovascular diseases, and instead focus on niche markets such as ophthalmology, dermatology, central nervous system (CNS), or rare diseases.
    • Representative companies: Santen (ophthalmology), Jazz Pharmaceuticals (rare diseases/sleep disorders).

III. Downstream: Distribution, Channels, and Payers (Commercial Monetization and Value Loop)

After drugs are produced, they must be delivered to end users through an efficient supply chain and monetized through a specific payment system.

  1. Pharmaceutical Distributors

    • Role: Warehousing, distribution, financing, and channel management between pharmaceutical companies and end users.
    • Business characteristics: Low gross profit margin (usually below 10%), and extreme reliance on economies of scale and efficient capital turnover.
    • Representative companies:
      • The three major US companies: McKesson, Centora/AmerisourceBergen, and Cardinal Health.
      • Chinese giants: Sinopharm, Shanghai Pharmaceuticals, China Resources Pharmaceutical, and Jointown Pharmaceutical Group.

  2. End Channels

    • Hospital Market (HCOs): Public/private hospitals. The core market for the vast majority of prescription drugs, injectables, and critical care medications.
    • Retail & DTP Pharmacies: Chain pharmacies, DTP (Direct to Patient) high-value prescription pharmacies, and online pharmacy platforms (such as JD Health and Meituan Pharmacy).

  3. Payers & Access – The Industry’s “Life and Death Power”

    • Single Payer Model (China/Most European Countries): Primarily led by the National Healthcare Security Administration (NHS/NMPA healthcare negotiations). The price of generic drugs is significantly reduced through centralized volume-based procurement, while innovative drugs are brought into hospitals through “medical insurance negotiations.”
    • Multi-payer model (US market): Commercial insurance companies (UnitedHealth, Anthem, etc.) and PBMs (Pharmacy Benefit Managers, such as CVS Caremark, Express Scripts) hold the power of life and death over whether a drug can be included in the reimbursement list. PBMs, by controlling a massive number of patient payments, force pharmaceutical companies to offer substantial rebates.

IV. The Underlying Operating Mechanism of the Industry Chain: “Patent Cliff” and Value Cycle

The flow of funds and value throughout the pharmaceutical industry chain is driven by the lever of “patent protection.”

New drug R&D investment -> Patent monopoly period (extremely high gross profit margins and increased sales volume) -> Patent expiration/patent cliff (price plummets by 90%) -> Funds return -> Investment in next-generation innovative drugs or acquisitions of biotechnology companies.

  1. Biotech companies undertake high-risk breakthroughs in the early stages;
  2. CXOs earn fixed service fees;
  3. Big Pharma leverages its vast capital and business networks to launch new drugs globally, maximizing profits during the 10-15 year patent term;
  4. After patent expiration, generic drug companies and distributors quickly take over the market, significantly reducing healthcare costs, while Big Pharma uses its profits to seek the next biotech target, completing the cycle.

Of course, high returns often come with high risks. This complex competitive landscape limits drug development efficiency and triggers various commercial struggles and public health crises.

Part 4: Industry Issues and Challenges

1. The “Eroom’s Law” of R&D Efficiency

Exponentially Soaring R&D Costs: Contrary to Moore’s Law in the semiconductor industry, the pharmaceutical industry faces an “Eroom’s Law”—the number of new drug approvals that can be obtained for every $1 billion invested is roughly halved every 9 years.

  1. Low-hanging fruit has been picked clean: Simple targets and easily druggable small molecules have been fully developed. New drug development now faces the “double ten rule” (10 years of time and $1-2 billion in investment), and the overall failure rate of clinical trials still exceeds 90%.

2. Commercial Incentives and Public Health Needs: Severe Mismatch in Public Health Needs

“Controlling Disease” is More Profitable than “Cure Completely”: Driven by capital, R&D funding is highly concentrated on chronic diseases requiring lifelong medication (such as blood sugar lowering, weight loss, and blood lipid lowering) and expensive cancer drugs, while areas of extreme societal need have stagnated:

  • New Antibiotic Crisis: Given the small dosage, short duration of use, and ease of developing drug resistance, pharmaceutical companies cannot recoup their R&D costs, leading to the bankruptcy of many biotech companies developing antibiotics. The world is facing the threat of untreatable superbugs.
  • Rare and Tropical Diseases Forgotten: Diseases with extremely small patient populations or very low affordability are unlikely to attract commercial R&D funding.

3. Distorted Intermediary Rent-Seeking and High Drug Prices

Kickback Mechanisms Inflate Drug Prices: Taking the world’s largest market, the United States, as an example, Pharmacy Benefit Managers (PBMs) control the power to include drugs in insurance reimbursement lists. PBMs (Pharmacy Benefit Managers) often favor drugs with extremely high list prices and large rebate margins, forcing pharmaceutical companies to continuously raise initial drug prices. The vast majority of the price premium is taken by intermediaries, and the burden is ultimately passed on to patients.

4. Geopolitics and Global Supply Chain Vulnerability

High Concentration of Active Pharmaceutical Ingredients (APIs): Global production capacity for basic APIs and key intermediates is highly concentrated in China and India. Against the backdrop of escalating geopolitical competition, the risk of decoupling and policy restrictions (such as the Biosafety Act) increase the potential for supply chain disruptions and drive up restructuring costs for global pharmaceutical companies.
