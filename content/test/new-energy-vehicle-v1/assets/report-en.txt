Electric mobility has reached substantial scale, but growth, profit and dependable service are different achievements. This report follows a fast-charging launch through industry history, regional markets, the value chain and company economics. Its central assessment is that competitive strength depends on turning technology into repeatable manufacturing, affordable ownership and reliable energy access, with returns evaluated within each business and statistical boundary.

Global research with emphasis on China, Europe and North America. Passenger BEVs and PHEVs, including EREVs where included by the source, form the core. Commercial vehicles and fuel-cell vehicles are separate context. Public information available through 18 October 2026. The SNE group share chart explicitly covers a broader BEV plus PHEV market including commercial vehicles in 80 countries. Complete-year 2025 results and 2026 interim observations remain separate.

Part 1  Industry Story

On 17 March 2025, BYD introduced its Super e-Platform in China with a claim designed to make charging feel familiar: the equipped Han L could add 400 kilometres of range in five minutes. The announcement described a 1,000-volt architecture and peak charging power of 1,000 kilowatts. Han L and Tang L models using the platform entered pre-sales. These were manufacturer specifications and launch claims, rather than measurements of the experience available to every electric-car owner. [H01]

The distinction matters. A peak power rating describes a moment in a charging session. Energy delivered depends on the power maintained over time. NREL's road-trip research illustrates how charging power changes with a battery's state of charge; its modelled curves reduce power as the battery becomes fuller. The study does not test BYD's technology, but it explains why the headline power alone cannot determine the duration of an entire charging session. [H02]

BYD's announcement does not provide a complete test protocol covering battery temperature, starting and ending charge levels, and the basis for converting the added energy into kilometres. Its five-minute figure should therefore remain attributed to the company. It cannot be read as a promise of the same usable distance in every season, on every road or from every public charger. [H01]

The same launch also pointed beyond the car. BYD announced a dedicated charging terminal and plans to build more than 4,000 megawatt flash-charging stations in China. That was a construction ambition, rather than confirmation of an operating network. Official US charging guidance similarly treats a station as a project involving electrical capacity, installation, permitting and ongoing operation, with utilities involved early in planning. [H01][H03]

The commercial question is how much of an impressive vehicle capability can become a dependable service. For a driver, the useful result is a convenient stop at an accessible, working station. For an operator, the equipment must earn enough over its life to support construction and operating costs. Our analysis therefore follows the complete system: the battery and vehicle, the charging site, the electricity connection and the customer paying for transport. A faster component can improve that system, but measuring the component does not measure the whole service. This relationship has shaped electric mobility for much longer than the current charging race.

Part 2  Industry History

1. Nineteenth century to the 1930s  an early transport option encounters a larger system

Electric propulsion emerged from a sequence of nineteenth-century advances in batteries and motors. The US Department of Energy's historical account cautions against assigning the electric car to one inventor or country. Gaston Planté's rechargeable lead-acid battery, dated to 1859 by the Science Museum, was one important storage milestone. It was not a ready-made solution to every requirement of road transport. [H04][H05]

In the United States around the turn of the twentieth century, electric cars appealed to urban users because they were quiet and relatively easy to operate. Their competitors also improved. The DOE account connects the expansion of gasoline cars with lower vehicle prices, electric starting, roads between cities and the availability of fuel. These changes affected the convenience and cost of owning a vehicle, beyond the merits of its motor. This is a US-centred history, rather than evidence that every country followed an identical path. [H04]

The lesson for the present industry is a systems question. A vehicle competes within a network of energy supply, maintenance, finance and everyday travel needs. Our interpretation is that adoption becomes harder when improvements in one element depend on complementary investments elsewhere. The early history does not prove that electric cars were inherently unsuitable; it shows why a promising propulsion technology alone could not settle the transport market.

2. The 1970s to the 1990s  battery research and air-quality regulation reopen the opportunity

Oil-supply concerns renewed US interest in alternative propulsion during the 1970s. The 1976 Electric and Hybrid Vehicle Research, Development, and Demonstration Act authorised federal support. This did not immediately establish a broad consumer market. [H04]

At the same time, rechargeable-battery research was changing the technical possibilities. The Nobel Prize account traces an important sequence through Stanley Whittingham's lithium-battery research in the 1970s, John Goodenough's higher-voltage oxide cathode work in 1980 and Akira Yoshino's carbon-based anode configuration in 1985. It dates the commercial sale of lithium-ion batteries to 1991. These developments were among the foundations later used in electric vehicles; they did not mean that an affordable, durable automotive battery pack appeared fully formed in 1991. The 2019 chemistry prize recognised the three scientists' contributions without providing a complete inventory of everyone involved. [H06]

Regulation created a second route back to the vehicle market. California's regulator dates its zero-emission vehicle regulation to 1990. GM's own retrospective describes the Impact concept in that year and EV1 leasing during the 1990s. The programme offered an operating vehicle to customers, although a limited leasing programme was different from a broadly established retail market. [H07][H08]

These developments illustrate two separate hurdles: a usable technology and an organisation capable of producing, supporting and selling it. Demonstrations could establish experience and expose problems. They could not, by themselves, establish that manufacturing costs, customer demand and supporting infrastructure would scale together. That distinction remains relevant when assessing new battery or vehicle announcements.

3. From 2008 into the 2010s  production cars and industrial policy develop together

Tesla's Roadster provided a visible commercial milestone. Its production announcement states that regular production began on 17 March 2008, following a prototype unveiled in 2006. The IR archive displays the release one day earlier; this report uses the event date in its dateline. The vehicle showed a route into the market through an electric sports car, without establishing that electric motoring was already affordable for the general population. [H09]

An established manufacturer took a different product route. Nissan's February 2011 results statement confirms that LEAF production began at Oppama in October 2010 and deliveries to customers in Japan and the United States began that December. These are production and delivery events, rather than merely an announced future launch. [H10]

China was developing an adoption and industrial framework alongside these product programmes. A January 2009 notice established an initial demonstration pilot in 13 cities, concentrating on public-service uses such as buses, taxis, official vehicles, sanitation and postal services. Its historical category included conventional hybrids and fuel-cell vehicles as well as battery-electric vehicles. It must not be treated as an early sales total for this report's narrower plug-in passenger-car market. [H11]

The State Council's 2012–2020 plan then selected electric drive as the main strategic direction and prioritised industrialisation of BEVs and PHEVs. It also addressed batteries, motors, electronics, charging, after-sales support and recycling. The document expressed objectives and policy tasks, not proof that every target was achieved. [H12]

Taken together, these milestones show several ways of creating a market: a specialist product, an established manufacturer's production system and coordinated public procurement. Their relative contributions cannot be reduced to a single invention or subsidy. In our reading, the emerging competitive task was to connect product development with repeatable manufacturing and an environment in which customers could operate the vehicles.

4. From 2020 to 2025  policy meets scale, with different regional outcomes

Around 2020, policy increasingly affected manufacturers' wider fleets. The European Commission describes tighter fleet-wide CO2 targets applying from 2020 as requiring better efficiency and faster deployment of low- and zero-emission vehicles. This was an average-fleet compliance mechanism, rather than a requirement that every car have identical emissions or an immediate prohibition of gasoline-car sales. [H15]

In April 2020, China extended its purchase-support programme through the end of 2022 with scheduled reductions. Its longer-term 2021–2035 industry plan, issued in October and published in November 2020, also emphasised market mechanisms, enterprise choices and integration with electricity and information infrastructure. Charging, swapping, safety, standards and recycling remained part of the policy agenda. A timetable for purchase-subsidy reductions did not mean all industrial and demand-side support would disappear. [H13][H14]

By 2025 the market had reached substantial scale. The IEA's May 2026 assessment estimates that global BEV and PHEV car sales exceeded 20 million in 2025, increasing 20% and reaching 25% of new-car sales. Penetration was nearly 55% in China, 28% in Europe and just under 10% in the United States. These are shares of new passenger-car sales; they are not shares of all vehicles already on the road, and Europe is not synonymous with the European Union. [H16]

The historical result is a large market with distinct regional conditions. Our analysis should therefore test affordability, policy exposure, charging access and manufacturing economics within each market. Global growth is useful context, but it does not establish that every manufacturer, battery chemistry or charging business will earn an adequate return. The next part examines where value is created along that industrial chain.

Part 3 Market Landscape

1 Define the market before comparing its size

The core market in this report is passenger vehicles that can be charged from the electricity grid: battery electric vehicles (BEVs) and plug-in hybrids (PHEVs). Extended-range electric vehicles (EREVs) sit within the plug-in family where the source includes them; they are not added again to an inclusive PHEV total. Conventional hybrids without external charging are an adjacent competing technology. Fuel-cell vehicles and commercial vehicles are discussed separately. China's regulatory category of new energy vehicles is broader than this passenger-car core. [M01][M11]

A sale can mean a manufacturer shipment, delivery to an end customer or registration. Each answers a different question. Wholesale indicates the manufacturer's channel flow; domestic retail is closer to local consumer demand; registration is an administrative observation. None is automatically revenue recognised in audited accounts. Likewise, a company's sales growth and the growth of an industry with a different geographical or product boundary cannot be directly combined into a market share.

IEA's 2025 assessment describes a mass market with considerable regional differences. Its electric cars were 25% of new car sales; BEVs represented 65% of electric car sales. The table gives sales estimates within the same framework and retains their published precision. [M01]

The difference from IEA's European total is not evidence that either source is wrong: EU membership and IEA's European coverage are different boundaries. [M07]

2 The 2026 market is diverging across regions

IEA's May 2026 report recorded about 3.9 million electric car sales in the first quarter, down 8% year on year. Its forecast at that time was 23 million for the full year, or 28% of new car sales. The forecast is a dated expectation, not an achieved annual total or an October update. Short-term weakness and a larger annual market are therefore different claims. [M02]

China illustrates why penetration and volume should be read together. CADA's passenger-car committee reported 7.758 million domestic NEV passenger-car retail sales in January to September 2026, down 12.8%, while manufacturer wholesale reached 11.448 million, up 9.4%. September retail penetration was 67.0%, yet NEV retail volume declined 16.7% year on year. Wholesale includes channels that domestic retail does not; subtracting the two does not directly measure inventory. [M06]

In the EU, January to August 2026 registrations reached 1,641,333 BEVs and 758,082 PHEVs. Their shares of all new cars were 21.7% and 10.0%; conventional hybrids had a separate 36.6% share. Including the latter in plug-in adoption would materially overstate the market being studied. These eight-month results must not be treated as full-year figures. [M08]

North America also needs disaggregation. US demand is especially exposed to changes in federal purchase incentives, but Canadian fleet statistics describe a separate market. Statistics Canada recorded 25.3 million light-duty vehicles in use in 2025; BEV plus PHEV vehicles represented 3.5% of that stock. This is not new-sales penetration, and its light-duty category includes pickups and vans. Mexico's importance as a manufacturing location should likewise not be mistaken for the size of its domestic electric passenger-car market. [M09][M10]

The analytical implication is that there is no single regional timetable. A manufacturer can face rising electric penetration, falling domestic unit demand and growing export shipments simultaneously. Regional capacity plans should be tested against local affordability, financing, electricity access and compliance requirements, rather than against one global growth rate.

3 The value chain connects materials to lifetime vehicle service

Figure 1 maps the passenger-vehicle value chain. Its links show categories, not verified supplier contracts. Companies may operate in several stages: BYD is an example in both batteries and vehicles. Listed companies are illustrative; the map is not a ranking. Company financial boundaries are discussed below. [M03][M19][M20][C01][C03][C07][C09][C11][C12]







Upstream materials include lithium salts, graphite and cathode, anode, electrolyte and separator products. Their customers need repeatable quality as well as available tonnes. For a vehicle programme, an inexpensive material that cannot meet production consistency and qualification requirements is not an interchangeable input. The economic question is therefore how much saleable, qualified material a process can supply at a given cost, rather than how much nominal capacity has been announced.

Cells and packs convert those inputs into a component with energy, power, thermal and lifetime constraints. CATL, BYD, LG Energy Solution and Panasonic illustrate this stage. IEA puts global EV battery deployment at 1.2 TWh in 2025 and LFP's share above 55% by deployed capacity. These are battery-energy measures, not numbers of vehicles. Nameplate battery manufacturing capacity above 4 TWh cannot be divided into deployment to obtain a valid factory utilisation rate: applications, inventories, production and installation timing differ. [M03]

Power electronics and control connect the battery to useful motion. The traction inverter controls motor torque and speed; Infineon's portfolio illustrates silicon and silicon-carbide devices and control electronics used in this category. The system choice involves efficiency, cost, thermal design and reliability. A component supplier's claimed performance improvement is not automatically an independently measured benefit in every finished vehicle. [M19]

Midstream vehicle companies integrate powertrains, structures, battery management, cooling, software and manufacturing. A dedicated electric platform can create design freedom, but it also requires development and tooling investment. Sharing parts across models can spread that spending; excessive complexity can consume the saving. Established groups must manage electric programmes alongside existing products and factories, while newer firms must build quality control, distribution and service capacity as their fleets expand.

Downstream, selling the car creates a continuing service obligation. Dealers and direct sales organise delivery, financing, repairs and used-car resale in different ways. Leasing shifts attention to residual values and battery condition. A lower new-car price can improve affordability while reducing the resale value of existing vehicles, changing the economics of the same fleet contract.

Charging is a related infrastructure business with several participants: equipment suppliers, site owners, network operators, electricity suppliers and payment or roaming providers. ABB illustrates AC and DC charging equipment supply. These roles should not be collapsed into one charging-market share. An equipment order, an installed connection and an operating charging point are different milestones. [M20]

Collection, testing, reuse and material recovery form the circularity stage. A retired pack must be classified before a decision about repair, second use or recycling. The value of recoverable materials must cover collection, transport, processing and compliance costs. Recycling is therefore an operating business whose economics depend on feedstock and recovery quality, rather than simply a consequence of selling more new cars. [M14]

4 Global production and global ownership tell different stories

IEA estimates nearly 22 million electric cars were produced in 2025. China accounted for nearly 75% of production and roughly one-quarter of global production was traded internationally. A car made in China may carry a non-Chinese brand and be sold elsewhere. Production location, brand ownership and final customer location must remain separate measures. [M05]

Exports can extend a manufacturer's addressable demand and spread platform spending. They also introduce freight, tariffs, local homologation, distribution, working capital and warranty costs. Announcing a factory abroad does not prove local output, and shipping a car overseas does not prove a final customer registration. In this report's analysis, durable international expansion requires evidence that local service, parts supply and customer economics remain viable after the initial shipment.

The selected companies illustrate different ways of competing in electric vehicles. BYD combines vehicle manufacturing with substantial internal component capabilities. BYD  integration supports execution but does not guarantee profit growth

BYD reported 4.545 million passenger-vehicle sales in 2025. Its broader automotive-related product category generated RMB648.65 billion of revenue and a 20.49% gross margin. Group attributable net profit fell 18.97% to RMB32.62 billion. These are different accounting boundaries: the product category includes automotive-related and other products, while group profit also includes electronics and other activities. The evidence supports a narrower conclusion than “scale guarantees earnings”: a large vehicle operation can coexist with declining group profit. [C01]

The first half of 2026 reinforces the need to inspect several measures together. Automotive-related revenue fell 8.98% to RMB275.34 billion while its gross margin rose to 22.33%; group attributable profit nevertheless fell 20.54%. Revenue, product gross margin and group net profit can move in different directions. The report does not attribute this entire gap to vehicle pricing, so neither does this analysis. [C02]

Analytical implication: internal batteries, motors, controls and chips can make design changes and cost coordination easier. They also place more production assets, development work and utilisation risk inside the company. Integration changes where risks are managed; demand still determines how productively those assets operate.

Tesla  distinguish automotive economics from the wider company

Tesla’s 2025 automotive revenue was USD69.53 billion, including USD1.99 billion of regulatory credits and vehicle leasing. Automotive gross margin was 17.8%, while consolidated operating income was USD4.36 billion on USD94.83 billion of revenue. The latter implies a calculated group operating margin of about 4.6%, rather than a passenger-vehicle segment margin. Tesla identifies incentives, mix and lower cash deliveries among the pressures on automotive revenue. It also names Panasonic and CATL as cell suppliers alongside its efforts to manufacture cells internally. [C03]

In H1 2026, automotive revenue reached USD36.75 billion and automotive gross margin increased to 18.7%, from 16.8% a year earlier. Yet Q2’s corresponding margin fell to 16.9%, from 17.2%. These statements are consistent because one measures six months and the other three. The company also notes the prior-year Model Y factory changeovers affected the comparison base. A rebound should therefore be examined over several periods, rather than extrapolated from a single percentage. [C04]

Volkswagen  electrification takes place within a large existing system

Volkswagen’s 2025 consolidated revenue was EUR321.91 billion, with operating profit of EUR8.87 billion and a 2.8% operating return on sales. These are whole-group figures, including activities outside electric passenger cars. Its disclosed 983,120 BEV deliveries explicitly include heavy commercial vehicles. That number is useful evidence of electrification within the group, but cannot enter this report’s passenger-car market-share calculation. [C07]

H1 2026 revenue was EUR158.10 billion and operating profit EUR5.93 billion, compared with EUR158.36 billion and EUR6.71 billion respectively in H1 2025. [C08] The analytical issue for an established group is the allocation of cash and engineering capacity across current products and future platforms. Existing factories, distribution and service networks offer capabilities; adapting them also consumes resources. The consolidated margin alone cannot identify the profitability of its BEV programme.

Geely Auto  rapid electrification requires a precise corporate boundary

Geely Auto reported 2025 revenue of RMB345.23 billion and attributable profit of RMB16.85 billion. Its reported NEV sales comprised 1,073,805 BEVs and 613,962 PHEVs. The listed group’s accounts include Lynk & Co, with the relevant comparisons restated; this is not the broader Geely Holding portfolio. [C09] In H1 2026 it reported RMB173.60 billion of revenue, RMB9.09 billion of attributable profit and 799,454 NEV sales. [C10]

For a multi-brand manufacturer, shared engineering can spread development costs over more products. Brand proliferation also requires clear product positioning: several badges do not automatically create several distinct sources of demand. This is an analytical trade-off, rather than an assertion that the accounts isolate the financial effect of each brand.

Hyundai and Kia  powertrain flexibility and the cost of transition

The companies’ separate 2025 results releases reported Hyundai Motor revenue of KRW186.25 trillion and operating profit of KRW11.47 trillion, and Kia revenue of KRW114.1 trillion and operating profit of KRW9.08 trillion. Hyundai’s release identifies its financial figures as preliminary and unaudited. Both companies’ broader electrified-vehicle totals include ordinary hybrids, so those totals cannot represent this report’s BEV/PHEV market. Hyundai Motor Company and Kia also remain separate reporting entities, rather than a consolidated Hyundai Motor Group financial statement. [C11][C12]

The strategic comparison is therefore between portfolios and operating capabilities, not a contest between headline “electrified” volumes. A hybrid can serve buyers facing charging constraints while generating cash for future vehicles. It also requires production and development resources. Flexibility can help a company adapt to uneven regional demand, but sustaining several technologies increases coordination requirements. Group profitability does not establish which powertrain earns the return.

CATL  the supplier profit pool must be examined separately

CATL’s 2025 results announcement, labelled unaudited, reported EV-battery revenue of RMB316.51 billion, with a 23.84% gross margin. Its overall gross margin was higher at 26.27%, reflecting a wider group that also includes storage batteries, materials, recycling and other activities. Battery-system economics therefore cannot be inferred from the group margin alone, and GWh cannot be converted into a vehicle market share without additional vehicle-level evidence. [C05]

In H1 2026, EV-battery-system revenue grew 46.02% to RMB192.12 billion, while the product gross margin fell 1.78 percentage points to 20.63%. Growth and margin expansion were not synonymous. [C06] A specialist supplier can distribute research and manufacturing costs across several customers, but must still manage pricing, product mix, capacity and customer concentration. These mechanisms explain why purchasing from a specialist can remain rational even for an automaker that designs important systems internally.

What the comparison means for industry structure

The central decision is how to organise interfaces between the vehicle, battery, software and manufacturing process. Internal production can improve coordination where system design is tightly connected. External procurement can provide access to specialist capabilities and reduce the amount of capital an automaker must commit to every component. Neither arrangement removes the need for supplier qualification, testing, warranty support or credible production planning.

The same logic applies geographically. A successful product has to work within local charging access, distribution, service and production conditions. Moving assembly closer to customers can improve responsiveness, but a factory adds fixed costs before demand is certain. The relevant question is whether the operating system can support sustained sales at an adequate return. The disclosures above show why vehicle volume, product gross margin, group operating profit and cash generation should be examined together, with their definitions kept visible. They support an analysis of industrial organisation and competitive execution, not an investment recommendation.

Part 4 Industry Challenges

1 Improving affordability without transferring losses elsewhere

The customer's economic comparison includes purchase price, financing, energy, insurance, maintenance, taxes and resale value. A low electricity tariff alone cannot establish lower total ownership cost. A household able to charge overnight at home and one dependent on public fast charging face different bills and constraints. Similarly, a fleet's high annual mileage may improve the value of energy savings while increasing its exposure to downtime.

Price competition can reduce the entry price and expand access. It can also reduce the residual value of previously sold cars, weaken dealers and squeeze component suppliers. The relevant business test is whether the manufacturer can sustain quality, warranty service and future model investment after discounting. Lower materials prices or a favourable product mix can protect margins temporarily; neither establishes a permanent industry-wide cost advantage. The company disclosures in Part 3 show why group profit, automotive gross margin and battery segment margin must remain separate measures.

2 Charging performance is a system outcome

A charging-power peak is not the average power over a session. The practical stop also includes finding a compatible operating charger, connecting, payment and possible queues. Battery state of charge, temperature, vehicle limits and power sharing can change the result. As an engineering illustration, four vehicles simultaneously drawing 1 MW would require 4 MW before losses; this is arithmetic, not a measured site load or a claim that any named station supplies it.

IEA reports more than 7 million public charging points worldwide at end 2025, following nearly 1.8 million additions. Those counts do not establish station availability or driver experience. Operators need appropriate locations, grid connections, maintenance and utilisation; drivers need dependable access. Adding rated power without enough demand can leave an expensive asset underused, while high-demand locations can still experience queues. [M04]

The solution is a mix of home and workplace charging, well-sited public infrastructure, reliable payment and maintenance, and managed charging where drivers can shift demand. Battery swapping can shorten the stop for compatible fleets, but creates requirements for standardisation, spare-battery inventory, asset ownership and battery-health assessment. No one model removes the need to pay for energy and infrastructure.

3 Battery tradeoffs and manufacturing execution

LFP, nickel-based lithium-ion chemistries and emerging alternatives address different requirements. Pack engineering, thermal management and vehicle efficiency can matter as much as a cell's headline energy density. LFP's growing deployment does not establish that every application should use it; a larger or heavier pack can create different vehicle constraints. Conversely, a higher-energy cell does not establish a cheaper or safer finished system. [M03]

Manufacturers should be assessed through usable output, production consistency, warranty experience and the intended charging window, rather than through laboratory records alone. Solid-state and other battery announcements should be separated into research results, validated prototypes, pilot output and sustained commercial deliveries. This is an evaluation framework, not a forecast of which company or chemistry will win.

Safety is a lifecycle responsibility involving cells, pack design, software, repair and emergency response. China's mandatory GB 38031 2025 standard took effect on 1 July 2026. The standard's existence and implementation date do not prove a particular fleet's incident rate, and meeting a specified test does not eliminate all real-world risk. Claims comparing fire risk require compatible denominators, vehicle age, exposure and reporting systems. [M12]

4 Emissions depend on the vehicle and energy pathway

Zero tailpipe emissions do not mean zero life-cycle emissions. ICCT's 2025 assessment of medium cars sold in the EU estimated BEV emissions of 63 g CO2e/km versus 235 for gasoline cars, a 73% reduction using the study's projected EU electricity mix for 2025 to 2044. The result includes manufacturing and use, but is a model with stated assumptions. It is not a universal percentage for every car and country. [M13]

The practical implication is to consider vehicle size, battery production, electricity supply, lifetime mileage and actual energy consumption together. PHEVs also require attention to the fraction of driving actually done on electricity. A plug-in car routinely driven without charging should not be credited as if every kilometre were electric. Cleaner electricity and efficient vehicles can reinforce each other, while oversized vehicles can consume part of the benefit.

5 Resource resilience and recycling economics

Supply risk can arise from concentrated processing and qualified materials even when geological resources are widely distributed. Changing a battery chemistry can reduce dependence on one input without eliminating the need for other materials or manufacturing expertise. New regional factories require qualified equipment, labour, suppliers and customers; a nameplate-capacity announcement does not establish competitive output.

IEA's recycling assessment identifies manufacturing scrap as the dominant current battery feedstock, with end-of-life packs becoming more important as fleets age. A recycler therefore needs to distinguish a future retirement opportunity from today's accessible feedstock. Collection contracts, transport, battery health, recovery quality and commodity prices affect revenue. Reuse may extend an asset's life, but testing, integration and liability also carry costs. [M14]

6 Policy support can change both demand and production incentives

China's published purchase-tax framework moved from exemption capped at CNY 30000 per eligible NEV passenger vehicle in 2024 to 2025 to half-tax relief capped at CNY 15000 in 2026 to 2027. This is tax relief subject to eligibility, not a uniform cash payment. In the US, IRS guidance states that the relevant federal vehicle credits are unavailable for vehicles acquired after 30 September 2025; acquisition and subsequent placement in service are distinct conditions. Neither change describes every local incentive or manufacturing credit. [M11][M10]

The EU adopted flexibility allowing manufacturers' CO2 compliance to be assessed over 2025 to 2027. Separately, a March 2026 Council debate addressed a Commission proposal changing the longer-term 2035 framework. A proposal, an adopted compliance arrangement and a final enforceable rule are different stages. Long-term demand models should not treat every announcement as enacted law. [M16][M17]

Trade policy also affects where value can be captured. The Commission's January 2026 guidance on Chinese BEV price undertakings addresses import prices, channels, cross-compensation and investment, with offers assessed individually. It is not a general removal of trade remedies for all brands, and BEV treatment cannot automatically be applied to PHEVs. Local production can reduce some cross-border exposure while adding fixed costs and execution risk. [M18]

7 Software value requires clear responsibility

Software can improve battery management, diagnostics and product functions, but feature availability is not proof of paid recurring demand. NHTSA's Level 2 definition keeps the driver engaged and attentive while the system assists steering and acceleration or braking. Such assistance should not be described as unrestricted autonomous driving. Commercial assessment should also include validation, cybersecurity, recall support and responsibility when features fail. [M15]

8 Commercial vehicles and hydrogen need separate operating cases

IEA reports that electric truck sales exceeded 400000 in 2025 and reached 9% of all truck sales; nearly all were BEVs. This category includes medium and heavy freight trucks, with classification revisions that limit comparisons with older editions. Its economics depend on payload, route length, depot energy, turnaround time and financing, not passenger-car buying preferences. Predictable routes can support coordinated charging or swapping, while long-distance operations impose different constraints. [M22]

Fuel-cell vehicles use hydrogen to produce electricity onboard. The hydrogen production pathway affects upstream emissions, as the US Alternative Fuels Data Center explains. A fleet considering hydrogen must assess delivered fuel cost, station coverage, utilisation and reliability, alongside payload and duty cycle. It should not infer zero life-cycle emissions from water at the tailpipe or infer a mass passenger market from a demonstration programme. [M21]

9 What would demonstrate a stronger industry

The useful indicators are compatible terminal sales, repeatable manufacturing quality, service capacity, sound cash conversion and infrastructure that drivers can use reliably. For automakers, monitor the relation between deliveries, discounting, warranty costs and working capital. For suppliers, monitor qualified output and customer concentration. For charging operators, monitor actual energy delivered and availability alongside capital and connection costs. For recyclers, monitor accessible feedstock and recovered material quality.

This report's assessment is that electrification has reached substantial scale, while the ability to earn sustainable returns remains uneven. The next stage is best evaluated through these operating measures and regional conditions. Forecast sales, announced capacity, charging demonstrations and software roadmaps should remain separate from realised commercial performance.
