Global Healthcare Industry Report
Medicines Devices Care and Payment
DEX Research | v1.1 | 2026-10-26

A global industry framework with quantitative case studies from the United States and China. This is an industry overview; its quantitative findings do not represent every region or healthcare submarket. The industry boundary includes medicines, medical devices and diagnostics, research and manufacturing services, distribution, clinical care and payment. Consumer wellness, beauty products and general fitness are excluded. Research cutoff is 26 October 2026. Data periods differ and are identified locally. The global spending figure is an explicitly historical 2022 baseline, not the latest GHED observation or a 2026 market estimate.

DEX assessment: durable commercial value is most likely where a business can demonstrate clinical usefulness, obtain a workable payment route and deliver reliably. More demand or a larger revenue base alone does not establish stronger profits. The analysis below separates observed facts from mechanisms and research hypotheses.

Part 1 Industry Story

A factory interruption reaches the bedside

In late September 2024, Hurricane Helene flooded Baxter's North Cove manufacturing facility in North Carolina. On 12 October, the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention issued a health advisory about disruption to intravenous and peritoneal dialysis solutions from the site. The advisory warned that patient care could be affected and called on healthcare organizations to assess supplies and plan mitigation. [E01, E03]

The regulatory response shows how a manufacturing problem becomes a clinical and institutional problem. On 11 October 2024, the FDA said three additional products were in inadequate national supply: 70% dextrose IV solution, lactated Ringer's IV solution and peritoneal dialysis solution. Baxter later reported that the plant had regained pre-hurricane production levels in February 2025; its 13 May 2025 update said inventory had recovered and allocations had been removed for virtually all North Cove IV solution codes. Those statements describe different stages of recovery. They do not establish that every hospital or every fluid product normalized on the same date. [E02, E03]

An infusion fluid depends on more than its ingredients. The care pathway also needs validated manufacturing, packaging, regulatory release, transport, purchasing and clinicians who can administer it safely. If one essential input is unavailable, an otherwise well-equipped facility can lose usable treatment capacity. The industrial lesson is that the economic importance of an input can be much larger than its visible place in a hospital budget. That is DEX's interpretation of the incident, not a measured estimate of its financial losses.

The case introduces the central question of this report: who creates a treatment, who gets it to the patient, and who pays for it? A patient may be the user while an insurer or government is the payer, and the hospital may choose the product. These roles create several separate decisions between technical invention and realized revenue. Those decisions shape which products get used and which businesses collect revenue.

Part 2 Industry Value Chain

Upstream research inputs and development services

Upstream organizations supply the tools and evidence needed to create medical products. Research instruments, reagents and laboratory consumables help discover and characterize candidates. Clinical research services help design and execute studies, manage study data and prepare evidence. These suppliers sell mainly to pharmaceutical companies, biotechnology firms, research institutions and laboratories. Thermo Fisher Scientific is an illustrative supplier across both groups; its 2025 Form 10-K describes laboratory products, outsourced drug development, clinical research and commercial manufacturing, including the PPD and Patheon brands. [E08]

The commercial models differ. A reagent supplier may benefit from repeated orders after a workflow is established. An instrument supplier combines equipment sales with service and consumables. A contract research organization, or CRO, delivers projects and contracted services. As an analytical matter, recurring use can create switching costs, while dependence on a few customers or on research budgets can make demand uneven. Repeat purchasing is not automatically a subscription, and a signed development project is not a guarantee that the underlying medicine will succeed.

A useful supplier investigation asks what must be revalidated if a customer switches, how much revenue comes from consumables rather than equipment, and how cancellations affect a backlog. These are diligence questions, not reported answers for every company. An upstream supplier may serve non-medical customers too, so its total corporate revenue should not be presented as pure healthcare revenue.

Midstream manufacturing medicines and medical products

The midstream converts knowledge and materials into regulated, usable products. We place contract development and manufacturing organizations, or CDMOs, here because they develop processes and manufacture drug substance or finished products. The boundary overlaps with upstream development services. Lonza is an example: its 2025 annual-report strategy section describes mammalian manufacturing, highly potent active pharmaceutical ingredients and drug-product capabilities. The document also reports customer agreements and the integration of its Vacaville site. These disclosures demonstrate activity; they do not establish a supply contract between Lonza and every medicine company shown in our map. [E09]

Medicine developers earn revenue through product sales and, depending on the contract, collaboration payments, royalties or milestones. Lilly's Mounjaro and Zepbound are concrete product examples. For this group, clinical differentiation must survive manufacturing scale-up and the buyer's coverage decision. Intellectual property and clinical evidence can support commercial protection, while competition, patent expiry and net-price changes can erode it. These mechanisms should be assessed at the product and indication level. [E10]

Diagnostics and medical devices turn physical measurements or interventions into clinical decisions and treatment. Siemens Healthineers illustrates imaging, diagnostics and therapy-related equipment; its November 2025 strategy announcement described a new Precision Therapy grouping. Medtronic's current company facts describe cardiovascular, medical surgical and neuroscience portfolios. Baxter illustrates infusion products. These are business examples, not a ranked list of the largest suppliers. [E03, E11, E12]

Equipment economics depend on what happens after installation. Training, maintenance, compatibility and recurring consumables can matter as much as the initial sale. For a diagnostic system, a reader should check whether an additional test creates incremental reagent revenue and whether the hospital can obtain payment for that test. For an implant or surgical device, procedure volumes and clinical adoption matter. A machine installed, a product cleared for a particular use and a reimbursed procedure are three different milestones.

CDMO capacity also needs careful interpretation. A new facility can expand potential output, but revenue depends on qualification, customer programs, operating utilization and contract terms. Construction announcements should not be converted directly into current sales. Likewise, a manufacturing contract or a company's description of its capabilities does not establish the clinical effectiveness of a product.

Downstream distribution care and payment

Distribution connects manufacturers with pharmacies and care providers. McKesson's fiscal 2026 Form 10-K describes its North American Pharmaceutical business as providing distribution and logistics for branded, generic, specialty, biosimilar and over-the-counter medicines. Hospitals, pharmacies and other customers buy availability, purchasing and distribution capability as well as the physical product. Inventory financing, customer credit and the economics of a distribution contract therefore belong in the analysis. [E13]

Clinical providers combine staff, facilities, equipment, drugs and consumables into care. HCA Healthcare is a hospital and outpatient-services example. At this stage, useful operating measures include admissions, case mix, utilization, staffing expense and collections. Capacity has to be staffed and supplied to be usable. A provider can face higher revenue alongside higher labor or input costs, so activity and profit need separate examination. [E14]

Payment runs across the value chain even though the map places it next to downstream demand. Governments, insurance arrangements, employers and households finance care. UnitedHealthcare and Optum illustrate the overlap between insurance and healthcare services within one group. A payer influences access through coverage, networks, prices and payment rules; it is not simply another manufacturer selling a treatment. Patients may also pay directly, and not all care has insurance coverage. [E05, E15]

The chain is therefore an editorial organization of functions, not a sequence of verified contracts. Thermo Fisher spans research, development, diagnostics and manufacturing services; Optum spans services and care-related functions. Representative companies may appear across functions. The connecting lines in Figure 1 show classification only and must not be read as ownership, customer or supplier relationships.

Part 3 Market Landscape

Measure spending before assigning market shares

Healthcare spending provides a scale reference, but it is not the sum of all healthcare-company revenues. A medicine can appear in manufacturer sales, distributor revenue and a final care bill. Adding those transactions can count the same economic flow several times. Insurer revenue also includes funds subsequently paid to providers. A meaningful market-share denominator must specify the country, period, product or service, and whether the measure is expenditure, revenue, units or enrollment.

The WHO's 2024 expenditure report records US$9.8 trillion of global current health expenditure in 2022. This is a historical scale reference, excludes health capital expenditure and is not a 2026 estimate. The US CMS national accounts record US$5,278.6 billion of national health expenditure in 2024, up 7.2%, including investment. China's official 2025 statistical bulletin gives a preliminary estimate of CNY9,336.72 billion of total health expenditure. Those measures differ in year, currency and accounting boundary; they are not combined into a global league table or used to calculate a US share of the world. [E04, E05, E06]

A forecast is another category of evidence. A population projection or anticipated budget path can inform a scenario, but it is not an observed sale. The differing statistical boundaries do not support a single compound growth rate covering every healthcare business in this edition. Segment-level research should choose a consistent dataset before calculating historical growth, and should retain revisions and changes in definitions.

Care delivery accounts for much of the US spending base

Figure 2 uses one denominator throughout: US national health expenditure in calendar 2024. Hospital care was US$1,634.7 billion, physician and clinical services US$1,109.7 billion, and retail prescription drugs US$467.0 billion. Together, hospital and physician/clinical services represented about 52.0% of the total. The chart's remaining category is the residual after the three named categories and includes other care, administration, public health and investment. This is an expenditure composition, not supplier market shares. [E05]

Retail prescription drugs are a narrower category than all medicines used in the health system. Products used within hospital and other service settings can be included in the relevant service expenditure. Treating the retail figure as total pharmaceutical demand would misread the accounts. Similarly, national hospital spending cannot be used as the denominator for a multinational provider's total revenue without adjusting geography and included services.

DEX interpretation: service capacity, clinical workflows and payment operations deserve attention alongside new drug launches. A technology supplier's opportunity depends partly on whether its customer has the staff, workflow and budget to use the technology. The spending chart establishes where recorded expenditure sits; it does not by itself establish how profitable a supplier will be.

A defined insurance submarket shows concentration

Medicare Advantage, also called Medicare Part C, provides Medicare benefits through approved private health plans, generally combining hospital, medical and prescription drug coverage. It is one part of US Medicare. The following figure excludes Original Medicare and does not describe the entire employer-insurance market. [E22]

Figure 3 is deliberately limited to US Medicare Advantage enrollment in March 2026. KFF's own analysis of CMS administrative data reports shares of 26% for UnitedHealth Group, 20% for Humana, 12% for CVS Health, 6% for Kaiser Foundation Health Plan and 5% for Elevance Health. Our displayed remainder is 31%, calculated from those published rounded percentages. It is not a global healthcare share, a revenue share or a measure of clinical quality. [E07]

Enrollment concentration can affect the negotiating setting for providers and suppliers, but national shares do not describe every county or provider network. A company can seek a different balance of membership, premium income and medical costs. Scale therefore needs to be interpreted together with benefit design, geographic coverage and the cost of serving members. This is a commercial mechanism, not a claim that the largest plan necessarily delivers better care.

Competition and profits differ across functions

Competition in innovative medicines is organized around specific diseases, clinical outcomes and access. A diagnostic or device supplier competes through clinical performance, usability and its installed workflow. A distributor competes through purchasing, logistics and service reliability. A provider competes within a care network for clinical capability and patients, while a payer manages coverage and financial risk. These functions interact, but they should not be ranked on one unadjusted corporate-revenue chart.

Three reported examples illustrate the difference. Lilly reported 2025 revenue of US$65.179 billion and net income of US$20.640 billion; HCA reported revenue of US$75.600 billion and net income attributable to HCA of US$6.784 billion; UnitedHealth Group reported revenue of US$447.6 billion and a 2.7% net margin. The first two corresponding income-to-revenue ratios are approximately 31.7% and 9.0%, calculated by DEX. They are company examples with different business scope and income attribution, not directly comparable segment margins or a representative industry average. [E10, E14, E15]

The medicine example also separates revenue growth from price increases. Lilly reported 45% revenue growth in 2025, with a disclosed breakdown of 50 percentage points from volume, minus 6 from price and plus 1 from foreign exchange, all rounded company figures. Mounjaro and Zepbound together contributed US$36.507 billion, about 56.0% of group revenue. That is corporate revenue concentration, not worldwide pharmaceutical market share. DEX assessment: growth quality requires examining the durability of volume, net-price changes and dependence on the main brands. [E10]

McKesson offers another useful distinction: its North American Pharmaceutical segment reported a 1.09% operating profit margin for the fiscal year ended 31 March 2026. A high value of goods distributed can support very large revenue without a similarly large percentage margin. Segment operating profit is different from group net income. Researchers should retain the company's exact profit definition rather than compare that 1.09% directly with the net-income ratios above. [E13]

The practical lesson is to locate the source of economic protection. A supplier may rely on process validation, a medicine on clinical evidence and intellectual property, a hospital on local capacity and specialist teams, and a payer on contracting and risk management. Each protection has a failure mode: replacement products, a failed trial, reimbursement changes, staff shortages or a customer switching. Valuation requires evidence about that particular business rather than an assumed healthcare premium.

China and ageing change the research questions

China's 2025 bulletin records 10.65 billion diagnosis and treatment visits across healthcare institutions, including 4.67 billion at hospitals. These are encounters, not unique patients. Within hospital visits in that same year, public hospitals recorded 3.93 billion and private hospitals 0.74 billion: approximately 84.2% and 15.8% respectively (Figure 4). The calculation uses published rounded visit totals; the shares do not measure revenue, profit or clinical quality. [E06]

The same bulletin lists 26,481 private hospitals out of 38,159 hospitals, about 69.4% by facility count. This is substantially above their share of visits. The measures describe different objects: the number of facilities and the volume of service activity. DEX assessment: counting hospitals alone does not establish their scale of care, and these shares do not by themselves establish differences in efficiency or quality. [E06]

Payment mechanisms also shape purchasing. The 2024 DRG/DIP explanation described case grouping and exceptional-case review. At its 2 September 2026 press conference, China's National Healthcare Security Administration announced version 3.0 and explained that payment standards use historical costs for patient groups. Savings from appropriate care can partly be retained under local arrangements, while the agency explicitly rejected requiring every case to remain below the standard. DEX assessment: suppliers need evidence about clinical value and delivery cost in the intended setting, alongside equipment installations and unit prices. Actual payment depends on the region, case and contract. [E19, E23]

The WHO projects that one in six people globally will be aged 60 or older by 2030. This supports investigating chronic care, rehabilitation and care coordination, but it does not establish a guaranteed revenue growth rate for any supplier. Need becomes commercial demand only when care is accessible, affordable and actually delivered. A useful research project follows that conversion rather than multiplying a population estimate by a product's advertised price. [E18]

Part 4 Industry Problems

Affordability constrains effective demand

The WHO and World Bank's 2025 monitoring report estimates that 4.6 billion people were not fully covered by essential health services in 2023, while 2.1 billion experienced financial hardship from out-of-pocket health spending in 2022. The years and populations differ; the figures should not be added. The report uses updated indicators, so comparisons with older headline estimates require a consistent series. Unmet need is therefore substantial, but it is not automatically a paying customer base. [E16]

For suppliers, the commercial constraint is the gap between a clinically desirable treatment and a budget that can pay for it. For households, the same gap can delay or prevent access. Possible responses include better pooling of financial risk and evidence-based purchasing. Each response requires financing, implementation and monitoring. Lowering one price alone does not ensure that personnel, diagnostics and follow-up become available.

Workforce capacity limits delivery

The WHO projects a shortfall of 11 million health workers by 2030, concentrated mostly in low- and lower-middle-income countries. Its workforce page also notes that unemployment can coexist with unmet needs where public systems cannot afford to employ trained people. The bottleneck therefore involves financing, distribution and retention as well as training. It should not be reduced to a universal shortage of doctors in every market. [E17]

More equipment can leave throughput unchanged if there are too few trained staff to operate it or too little capacity for follow-up. Scheduling tools, appropriate task allocation and training can be investigated as productivity interventions. Their value should be measured through safely completed care and staff workload, with attention to who bears the extra work. A shorter documentation task is useful only if the time saved survives integration and checking.

Reliability and clinical evidence remain costly

The Baxter interruption illustrates the exposure created when an essential input depends on a vulnerable production site. Additional qualified production, inventory and contingency planning can improve resilience, but each ties up resources. A purchaser choosing only the lowest quoted unit price may not be paying explicitly for reserve capacity. That is a procurement incentive to investigate, not proof that a specific purchasing contract caused this incident. [E01–E03]

Clinical evidence has a separate cost and uncertainty. A product must demonstrate value for a defined use and population; results from one setting do not automatically transfer to another. For drugs, changes in treatment alternatives or net prices can change the commercial return even after development. For devices, real clinical workflow and training affect adoption. Procurement should compare the total cost of safe delivery with the relevant outcome, while recognizing that evidence collection and longer follow-up also require money and time.

AI authorization does not establish commercial adoption

The FDA's AI-enabled medical-device list identifies devices authorized for marketing in the United States and links to device-specific entries. The agency explicitly says the list is not comprehensive. It is a record of identified authorizations, not a census of deployed systems, revenue, patients served or products proven to outperform clinicians in all settings. Counting entries and calling the result a market size would therefore be a category error. [E20]

The FDA's 2026 discussion paper on generative-AI medical devices considers risk assessment, premarket evaluation and postmarket monitoring. The agency states that it is for discussion only and is neither draft nor final guidance. That status matters: an open policy discussion must not be described as a new binding rule or a general authorization for autonomous diagnosis. [E21]

DEX's commercial test for an AI tool is specific: can a defined user complete a defined clinical or administrative task more safely or efficiently, after integration, human review and error handling? A credible evaluation should record performance on the intended population, the work transferred to staff, operating cost and the route to payment. Sales claims without that evidence remain hypotheses. A general-purpose model's impressive demonstration does not answer those questions.

Research priorities with observable tests

The preceding evidence suggests three research directions rather than a universal promise of rapid growth. First, examine critical-supply reliability: compare qualification, delivery performance and the cost of reserve capacity. Second, examine care productivity: compare actual time released and safely completed care with the full implementation cost. Third, examine evidence and payment operations: compare error rates, turnaround times and collected payments before and after a clearly defined intervention.

These are DEX research hypotheses, not measured market forecasts or recommendations to buy particular securities. For each opportunity, identify the budget owner, the purchasing decision, the contractual unit of revenue and an outcome that can be checked. An attractive healthcare business must connect a real patient or institutional need to an executable delivery model and a payment stream that can cover its costs.
