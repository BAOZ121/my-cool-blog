Oil and Gas Exploration and Production Industry Brief

Global markets and project economics

DEX Research | Review version 1.0 | 24 October 2026. Scope: global exploration and production (E&P), using full-year 2025 output as the comparable baseline. Developments are reviewed through 24 October 2026. Midstream and downstream explain the route to market.

Core view: E&P is a business of replacing production, managing subsurface uncertainty and recovering long-lived capital. Resource quality, access to market and lifetime costs reveal more about competitiveness than output growth alone. This is the author’s interpretation of the evidence below.

P1 Industry Story

On 8 August 2025, the ONE GUYANA floating production, storage and offloading vessel (FPSO) started producing oil offshore Guyana. ExxonMobil and the Guyanese government announced the startup. Its initial annual-average production target was 250,000 barrels a day; installed capacity across the relevant developments exceeded 900,000 barrels a day. Neither figure describes full-year 2025 actual production. [S02–S03]

The vessel makes an otherwise hidden industrial process tangible. A discovery still needs appraisal wells, development design, finance, equipment, subsea connections and commissioning before it produces a deliverable commodity. Resource discovery starts the process; execution, uptime and sales determine whether it generates cash.

On 4 February 2026, SBM Offshore announced the vessel’s sale for approximately US$2.32 billion in cash, while retaining operations and maintenance through 2035. This disclosed transaction shows how field interests, facility ownership and operating services can be separated. The vessel price is not the total cost of developing the field. [S04]

The resulting questions extend beyond how much oil the world needs: who obtains attractive resources, who delivers projects, and which assets can withstand lower prices, interruptions or delays?

P2 Industry Value Chain

Upstream is the focus: exploration, appraisal, development and production. Producers commit capital and bear geological and commodity-price risk, selling to refiners, traders or gas buyers. Aramco, ExxonMobil, the Guyana venture involving CNOOC, and independent E&P company ConocoPhillips illustrate different business models. [S02, S07, S09]

Suppliers provide tools and delivery capability. SLB offers reservoir characterisation, well construction, production and abandonment services; SBM supplies FPSO construction and operations. Equipment, service, lease and maintenance revenue must not simply be added to producers’ sales as a non-overlapping industry total. [S04, S06]

Midstream gathers, processes, transports and stores output; Enbridge illustrates pipelines and gas storage. LNG facilities connect gas to distant markets. Downstream turns crude into fuels and chemical feedstocks or delivers gas to power, industrial and household users. Aramco and ExxonMobil span multiple stages; Enbridge also distributes gas. [S02, S07–S08, S14]

P3 Market Landscape

Start with physical output. Global crude and condensate production averaged 85.760 million barrels a day in 2025; annual natural gas production was 4,196.5 billion cubic metres. Each chart uses its own global denominator from the same EI edition. These are production-location shares, not corporate revenue, reserves or export shares. [S01]

Competition operates at several levels. Integrated producers combine resource portfolios, execution and financing; independent E&P firms concentrate on upstream assets and capital allocation; service companies compete on technology, reliability and delivery. National output cannot be assigned to one national oil company, and operated output must not be confused with equity output. [S02, S06–S09; author’s classification]

Guyana illustrates ownership versus control. Disclosed Stabroek interests are ExxonMobil 45%, Hess 30% and CNOOC 25%. Chevron completed its Hess acquisition in July 2025, so Hess belongs within Chevron at group level; counting them as wholly separate competitors would misstate the landscape. [S02, S05]

Investment is also heterogeneous. In May 2026, the IEA projected annual oil investment below US$500 billion and gas investment around US$330 billion. These broader supply-infrastructure categories indicate capital allocation; they are neither E&P revenue nor a pure upstream capex total. [S12]

Understanding project returns

Realised prices multiplied by saleable equity output provide a starting point for producer revenue. Operating and transport costs, fiscal payments, capital spending, finance and retirement obligations then affect cash available. Production-sharing terms require separate treatment. This is an analytical framework, not an issuer’s accounting formula.

A barrel of oil equivalent (boe) converts energy, not sales value. Lifting cost is only part of total cost: it excludes burdens that can matter to new-project economics. Gas analysis must also examine transport capacity and charges; the consumer’s bill is not the producer’s wellhead revenue. [S14; author’s analysis]

P4 Industry Problems

Decline and capital discipline

Existing fields decline. The IEA estimates that nearly 90% of annual upstream investment since 2019 has offset declines. This helps explain substantial spending even without fast demand growth. Workovers, additional drilling and new projects can replenish supply, but spending does not establish economic value. Examine capital required for incremental output and subsequent operating performance. [S10]

Project timing and disrupted routes

Long procurement and construction cycles constrain responses to price increases. The IEA’s 2026 investment analysis identifies supply-chain and offshore-rig constraints; its October report forecasts broader oil supply of 100.7 mb/d and demand of 102.4 mb/d for 2026. These are dated forecasts, not a linear price signal or a series comparable with EI crude output. Alternative routes, inventory and staged development offer buffers at a cost. [S11–S12]

Methane and environmental obligations

The IEA estimates 2025 methane emissions of 45 Mt from oil and 36 Mt from gas activities. These scopes extend beyond E&P and are not wholly measured. Leak detection and repair, equipment replacement and reduced venting/flaring can help, but recovered gas still needs processing and market access. Retirement provisions belong in project design and funding, not only at the end of production. [S03, S06, S13]

Definitions and long-term demand

Resources, reserves, capacity, daily output and equity production answer different questions. An undefined market-size headline can obscure this. Near-term energy-security demand does not remove long-term substitution risk. The author’s view is to test lower prices, cost inflation and delays, matching demand assumptions to asset life rather than treating one high-price year as proof of durable profitability.

Monitoring priorities: comparable equity output and decline; project budgets and first-production dates; realised prices and transport differentials; sustaining versus growth capex; methane intensity and measurement coverage; and retirement cash obligations. This brief is an industry framework, not a company valuation or trading recommendation.

Calculations and review notes

Crude chart: crude, shale/tight oil, oil sands and condensates requiring further refining; excludes separately reported NGLs, biofuels and other synthetic liquids. Unit: thousand barrels/day; world denominator 85,760.

Gas chart: marketed production, excluding flared/recycled gas and including gas used for gas-to-liquids. Uses the source’s standardised volume basis; world denominator 4,196.5 bcm. One bcm is one billion cubic metres.

Share = country volume / same-table world volume × 100. Other is calculated from published volumes before share rounding. Independently rounded percentages can differ from 100% by 0.1 percentage point. Source values are already rounded; no additional precision is claimed.

The 2024–2025 gas annual-volume increase is approximately 1.29%; EI reports 1.6% on a leap-year-adjusted, daily-comparable basis. These are different measures. They are not interchanged in the body.

2026 forecasts retain their publisher, date and boundary. IEA total-oil balances are not spliced with EI crude/condensate data. No corporate-share chart is constructed from incompatible equity definitions.
