Jensen Huang is also identified as Jen-Hsun Huang in earlier university and company records. Born in Taiwan in 1963, he spent part of his childhood in Thailand. Around age nine, he and his older brother moved to the United States before their parents. Caltech's published biography records an initial stay with an uncle in Tacoma, Washington, followed by a move to Kentucky. He later reunited with his parents in Oregon as a teenager.<sup id="cite-2-1"><a href="#evidence-2" aria-label="Source 2">[2]</a></sup>

He has often recalled his childhood stay at Oneida Baptist Institute in Kentucky. The school's newsletter supplies a specific distinction: although the institute then operated principally as a high school, it allowed the young brothers to live on campus. They attended classes at nearby Oneida Elementary School. Their residence and the school where they took classes were separate institutions.<sup id="cite-3-1"><a href="#evidence-3" aria-label="Source 3">[3]</a></sup>

Public accounts establish the sequence of places, but retrospective descriptions do not consistently agree on exact early dates and ages. This biography therefore uses approximate ages rather than turning an uncertain recollection into a precise date.

## 2 Boarding life in Kentucky

At the NBA All-Star Technology Summit on February 14, 2025, Huang told interviewer Becky Quick that he had shared a dormitory room with a sixteen-year-old boy while he was much younger. He recalled that other boys worked on a tobacco farm, whereas he cleaned bathrooms because he was the youngest. He described the work as difficult and said he tried to perform his assigned tasks well.<sup id="cite-4-1"><a href="#evidence-4" aria-label="Source 4">[4]</a></sup>

These details come from Huang's later recollection. The school independently records the brothers' accommodation and elementary-school attendance.<sup id="cite-3-2"><a href="#evidence-3" aria-label="Source 3">[3]</a></sup><sup id="cite-4-2"><a href="#evidence-4" aria-label="Source 4">[4]</a></sup>

He remained connected to the institution. Its 2019 newsletter records a teaching and residential building supported by Huang and his wife, bringing a place from his childhood back into the documented account of his adult life.<sup id="cite-29-1"><a href="#evidence-29" aria-label="Source 29">[29]</a></sup>

## 3 Oregon high school, table tennis and restaurant work

After rejoining his parents, Huang attended Aloha High School in Oregon. HKUST's honorary doctorate citation records that he graduated at sixteen and had been a nationally ranked table-tennis player. The sport was table tennis, rather than tennis.<sup id="cite-5-1"><a href="#evidence-5" aria-label="Source 5">[5]</a></sup>

He also worked at Denny's, including washing dishes, clearing tables, and serving customers. NVIDIA's 2023 retrospective records this employment and his return to the San Jose restaurant associated with the company's founding for a commemorative event.<sup id="cite-10-1"><a href="#evidence-10" aria-label="Source 10">[10]</a></sup>

## 4 University studies and family life

Huang studied electrical engineering at Oregon State University, receiving his bachelor's degree in 1984. He earned a master's degree in electrical engineering from Stanford in 1992. Oregon State's alumni record notes that he completed his graduate studies while working full time; eight years separated the two degree dates.<sup id="cite-6-1"><a href="#evidence-6" aria-label="Source 6">[6]</a></sup>

His wife, Lori Huang, formerly Lori Mills, is also an Oregon State graduate. The university's 2022 engineering alumni magazine records that they met as laboratory partners in a first-year engineering course and married a few years later.<sup id="cite-7-1"><a href="#evidence-7" aria-label="Source 7">[7]</a></sup>

They have a son, Spencer, and a daughter, Madison. Huang recalled in 2010 that he continued taking Stanford classes while working, married, and raising children. His course load decreased after the children were born. He continued because he enjoyed studying and completed the master's degree over eight years.<sup id="cite-36-1"><a href="#evidence-36" aria-label="Source 36">[36]</a></sup>

## 5 An engineer at AMD and LSI Logic

{{< industry-map id="jensen-huang-career" >}}

Before founding NVIDIA, Huang worked at AMD and LSI Logic. Stanford's entrepreneurship program records his roles as a microprocessor designer at AMD and director of CoreWare at LSI Logic, positions in semiconductor design and related technology operations.<sup id="cite-8-1"><a href="#evidence-8" aria-label="Source 8">[8]</a></sup>

NVIDIA's other founders were Chris Malachowsky and Curtis Priem. Before the startup, they worked at Sun Microsystems while Huang worked at LSI Logic.<sup id="cite-8-2"><a href="#evidence-8" aria-label="Source 8">[8]</a></sup><sup id="cite-12-1"><a href="#evidence-12" aria-label="Source 12">[12]</a></sup>

By the time he completed his master's degree in 1992, Huang had already worked in industry. His earned academic qualifications when he began the company were a bachelor's and a master's degree in electrical engineering. His later honorary doctorates were honors conferred by universities.<sup id="cite-1-1"><a href="#evidence-1" aria-label="Source 1">[1]</a></sup><sup id="cite-6-2"><a href="#evidence-6" aria-label="Source 6">[6]</a></sup>

## 6 Three founders in 1993

NVIDIA's official history gives April 5, 1993, as its founding date and names Huang, Malachowsky, and Priem as the founders. Its initial focus was three-dimensional graphics for gaming and multimedia. Huang has served as president, chief executive officer, and a director since the company's inception.<sup id="cite-1-2"><a href="#evidence-1" aria-label="Source 1">[1]</a></sup><sup id="cite-9-1"><a href="#evidence-9" aria-label="Source 9">[9]</a></sup>

{{< biography-photo id="dennys" >}}

Some pre-founding discussions took place at a Denny's restaurant in San Jose, California. NVIDIA returned to the restaurant for a commemorative event in 2023. The restaurant was a meeting place; incorporation, financing, hiring, and product development involved additional steps.<sup id="cite-10-2"><a href="#evidence-10" aria-label="Source 10">[10]</a></sup>

In an April 2009 Stanford eCorner talk, Huang recalled treating his thirtieth birthday, February 17, 1993, as his first day working on the startup. He had not taken business or marketing classes and bought startup books and prepared financing presentation materials.<sup id="cite-11-1"><a href="#evidence-11" aria-label="Source 11">[11]</a></sup>

He also recalled hiring a lawyer who introduced the founders to venture investors. Investors considered their work history, qualifications, and market opportunity. The team began building the company without first completing a detailed business plan. These details come from his account of its first few months.<sup id="cite-11-2"><a href="#evidence-11" aria-label="Source 11">[11]</a></sup>

Huang took the CEO role, but the enterprise was founded by three partners. Subsequent chips, software, and systems were developed by engineering teams.

## 7 NV1, NV2 and the early product reversal

NVIDIA's first commercial chip, NV1, entered the market in 1995. The company's IPO prospectus states that it stopped selling NV1 in the first quarter of 1996 and abandoned NV2 development. RIVA 128, introduced in August 1997, used a subsequent graphics architecture.<sup id="cite-13-1"><a href="#evidence-13" aria-label="Source 13">[13]</a></sup>

In Sequoia's interview, Huang recalled that the original graphics approach diverged from the interfaces and architecture adopted by the broader market. He described asking Sega to release NVIDIA from an ongoing development arrangement. In his account, Sega agreed to relieve the contractual obligation while paying the original contract amount.<sup id="cite-12-2"><a href="#evidence-12" aria-label="Source 12">[12]</a></sup>

This negotiation story is a participant's later recollection. The biography does not treat every contractual detail, date, or payment as separately verified by the prospectus. The prospectus directly establishes the sequence of NV1 discontinuation, NV2 abandonment, and RIVA 128's introduction.<sup id="cite-12-3"><a href="#evidence-12" aria-label="Source 12">[12]</a></sup><sup id="cite-13-2"><a href="#evidence-13" aria-label="Source 13">[13]</a></sup>

As CEO, Huang participated in handling the product direction and customer relationship. The company was still private during this period, which included the discontinuation of its first product and cancellation of its next project.

## 8 The 1999 IPO and GeForce 256

NVIDIA went public on Nasdaq on January 22, 1999, under the ticker NVDA. Its investor FAQ gives an original IPO price of $12 per share, before subsequent stock-split adjustments.<sup id="cite-14-1"><a href="#evidence-14" aria-label="Source 14">[14]</a></sup>

That year, NVIDIA introduced GeForce 256 as a GPU. Its anniversary retrospective records October 11, 1999, as the release milestone and describes hardware transform and lighting that shifted graphics-processing work from the CPU.<sup id="cite-15-1"><a href="#evidence-15" aria-label="Source 15">[15]</a></sup>

The IPO and the product release were distinct events: one brought the company into the public capital markets; the other was a processor development. Both occurred during Huang's tenure, with the product's engineering performed by company teams.

## 9 CUDA and general-purpose parallel computing

NVIDIA's corporate history records the unveiling of CUDA architecture in 2006. CUDA provided a route for developers to program parallel computations on GPUs, extending their use beyond graphics rendering into scientific and other computing tasks.<sup id="cite-9-2"><a href="#evidence-9" aria-label="Source 9">[9]</a></sup><sup id="cite-16-1"><a href="#evidence-16" aria-label="Source 16">[16]</a></sup>

GPUs can execute large numbers of suitable calculations in parallel, but applications still require programs, tools, and algorithms. NVIDIA's CUDA developer materials provide tools, libraries, and programming resources. The company's work at this stage included both processor hardware and software support.<sup id="cite-16-2"><a href="#evidence-16" aria-label="Source 16">[16]</a></sup>

Huang continued as CEO. CUDA is a technology platform developed and maintained by NVIDIA teams.<sup id="cite-1-3"><a href="#evidence-1" aria-label="Source 1">[1]</a></sup><sup id="cite-16-3"><a href="#evidence-16" aria-label="Source 16">[16]</a></sup>

## 10 Deep learning and computing systems after 2012

{{< biography-photo id="gtc" >}}

In 2012, Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton published their image-classification neural-network paper. It describes using two NVIDIA GeForce GTX 580 GPUs and roughly five to six days to train the network. The architecture, training method, and experimental results were the researchers' work.<sup id="cite-17-1"><a href="#evidence-17" aria-label="Source 17">[17]</a></sup>


In April 2016, NVIDIA announced DGX-1, combining multiple GPUs and associated software in a deep-learning system. An August company account records Huang personally delivering a DGX-1 to OpenAI in San Francisco. These are product-launch and delivery events; the delivery alone does not establish that a later model was produced entirely on that machine.<sup id="cite-18-1"><a href="#evidence-18" aria-label="Source 18">[18]</a></sup><sup id="cite-19-1"><a href="#evidence-19" aria-label="Source 19">[19]</a></sup>

In March 2024, NVIDIA announced Blackwell, describing GPUs, CPU combinations, and networking components. Huang's public product presentations thus involved chips and larger computing systems, as well as personal-computer graphics cards.<sup id="cite-23-1"><a href="#evidence-23" aria-label="Source 23">[23]</a></sup>

## 11 Other events during his tenure

Early product reversals were not the company's only difficulties. NVIDIA's fiscal 2009 annual report disclosed a weak die/packaging material set in certain previous-generation notebook GPUs and related chips. The company recorded a $196 million charge for anticipated warranty, repair, return, replacement, and associated costs. This was a documented product-quality event during Huang's tenure.<sup id="cite-20-1"><a href="#evidence-20" aria-label="Source 20">[20]</a></sup>

On April 27, 2020, NVIDIA announced completion of its Mellanox acquisition, valued at approximately $7 billion. Mellanox's businesses included networking technology, which became part of NVIDIA following the transaction.<sup id="cite-21-1"><a href="#evidence-21" aria-label="Source 21">[21]</a></sup>

In February 2022, NVIDIA and SoftBank announced termination of NVIDIA's proposed Arm acquisition, citing significant regulatory challenges that prevented completion. The acquisition did not close, and Arm did not become a NVIDIA subsidiary through that transaction.<sup id="cite-22-1"><a href="#evidence-22" aria-label="Source 22">[22]</a></sup>

## 12 How he describes his management

Speaking at Stanford on January 29, 2003, Huang discussed corporate culture. He identified innovation, acknowledging mistakes, and self-criticism as elements of NVIDIA's culture and argued that a CEO should devote time to it. These were the management views he expressed at that time.<sup id="cite-24-1"><a href="#evidence-24" aria-label="Source 24">[24]</a></sup>

In a 2024 Stanford GSB interview, he described presenting employees with facts, data, assumptions, and unknowns, and explaining his reasoning. He said that fewer information-transfer layers and greater access to decision context helped employees make independent decisions. This is his description of his approach, rather than a survey of all employees' experiences.<sup id="cite-25-1"><a href="#evidence-25" aria-label="Source 25">[25]</a></sup>

In his November 23, 2024, HKUST conversation with Harry Shum, he again emphasized learning and accepting uncertainty. He said a leader need not know everything and could pursue a direction confidently while retaining room to learn. The university's account preserves the discussion.<sup id="cite-26-1"><a href="#evidence-26" aria-label="Source 26">[26]</a></sup>

In Stanford's 2010 interview, he recalled feeling let down when a younger manager resigned while he was managing a design center at twenty-four. Years later, he considered his reaction mistaken and said he should respect colleagues' freedom to choose other jobs.<sup id="cite-36-2"><a href="#evidence-36" aria-label="Source 36">[36]</a></sup>

## 13 Public appearances and family roles

Leather jackets recur in Huang's public appearances. HKUST's account of its November 2024 event records Huang and Shum wearing jackets bearing the university's name. A 2025 university donation announcement also records a charitable auction involving a jacket. The clothing appears in both event and fundraising records.<sup id="cite-26-2"><a href="#evidence-26" aria-label="Source 26">[26]</a></sup><sup id="cite-31-1"><a href="#evidence-31" aria-label="Source 31">[31]</a></sup>

His family also appears in company filings. NVIDIA's 2026 proxy statement discloses that his son and daughter work at the company, are not executive officers, and do not report directly to him. It describes their compensation arrangements. Those disclosures do not establish a family succession plan.<sup id="cite-35-1"><a href="#evidence-35" aria-label="Source 35">[35]</a></sup>

At this biography's cutoff, NVIDIA's official biography continues to identify Huang as a founder, president, CEO, and director. Product presentations are one part of his public work; the formal positions also involve ongoing management and board responsibilities.<sup id="cite-1-4"><a href="#evidence-1" aria-label="Source 1">[1]</a></sup>

Stanford's fiscal 2008 financial review records Huang and Lori's $30 million commitment for the new School of Engineering Center. A separate engineering-school account records the dedication on October 5, 2010. The center bears the name Jen-Hsun Huang.<sup id="cite-27-1"><a href="#evidence-27" aria-label="Source 27">[27]</a></sup><sup id="cite-28-1"><a href="#evidence-28" aria-label="Source 28">[28]</a></sup>

Oneida Baptist Institute's Fall 2019 newsletter records a $2 million matching grant from the couple for Jen-Hsun Huang Hall. The building contains thirty-two dormitory rooms accommodating up to 128 girls, plus twelve classrooms. It also records the first resident assistants serving in the building during the 2019–20 school year.<sup id="cite-29-2"><a href="#evidence-29" aria-label="Source 29">[29]</a></sup>

On October 14, 2022, Oregon State announced a $50 million gift from the couple to the university foundation for the Jen-Hsun and Lori Huang Collaborative Innovation Complex. The announcement lists research in AI, materials science, and robotics. This account records the announced gift and purpose; the projected opening date in that announcement is not treated as a confirmed completion date.<sup id="cite-30-1"><a href="#evidence-30" aria-label="Source 30">[30]</a></sup>

A September 2025 HKUST announcement records Terry Tsang's HK$10 million pledge at a charitable event and a further HK$10 million matching gift from the Jen-Hsun and Lori Huang Foundation for the Top Engineering Scholars Award. These records distinguish the donors—individual, couple, or foundation—and the currencies involved.<sup id="cite-31-2"><a href="#evidence-31" aria-label="Source 31">[31]</a></sup>

## 15 Honors, public responsibilities and the present

Huang entered Oregon State's Engineering Hall of Fame in 2013. On November 23, 2024, HKUST conferred an honorary doctorate in engineering. University biographies also record his election to the US National Academy of Engineering.<sup id="cite-5-2"><a href="#evidence-5" aria-label="Source 5">[5]</a></sup><sup id="cite-6-3"><a href="#evidence-6" aria-label="Source 6">[6]</a></sup>

The 2025 Queen Elizabeth Prize for Engineering recognized seven modern-machine-learning laureates: Bill Dally, Fei-Fei Li, Geoffrey Hinton, John Hopfield, Huang, Yann LeCun, and Yoshua Bengio. The prize organization identifies Huang and Dally's contribution with the hardware platforms supporting machine learning. Huang was one of seven recipients.<sup id="cite-32-1"><a href="#evidence-32" aria-label="Source 32">[32]</a></sup>

Huang received the 2026 IEEE Medal of Honor at the April 24 ceremony in New York City. On March 25 of the same year, the White House announced his appointment to the President's Council of Advisors on Science and Technology, whose role includes providing science and technology advice to the president.<sup id="cite-33-1"><a href="#evidence-33" aria-label="Source 33">[33]</a></sup><sup id="cite-34-1"><a href="#evidence-34" aria-label="Source 34">[34]</a></sup>

As of October 3, 2026, his public career continues. This biography records education, employment, founding, product events, philanthropy, and appointments already documented, without assigning a predicted ending to his career, wealth, or company.
